Knobs and a Score

Here is the actual thing first. A small network starts with random settings and sees two spirals of points, one arm per class. At first it is right on about half the points, a coin flip. After 1,000 steps it separates the spirals cleanly: accuracy climbed from 49 percent to 98 percent in a fraction of a second. Only 82 numbers moved, a little at a time, in a direction chosen by a rule, and this lesson explains that rule.

How can a network know anything? Picture a mixing desk in a music studio, where the sound depends on every knob position. A network is a long list of numbers, called parameters, and its answer depends on all of them. Learning means finding good knob positions, and for that we need a score for how far the sound is from the sound we wanted. That score is the loss, and smaller is better. Take one knob w, the rule y = 2x and the example x = 1, target 2. At w = 0 the prediction is 0, the miss is 2, and we square it to get a loss of 4. At w = 2 the loss is 0.

Each unit in a layer does three small jobs: it multiplies every input by its own weight, adds the products together with one extra number called the bias, and bends the result with ReLU, which keeps a positive number and replaces a negative one with 0. With inputs 0.5 and -1, a unit with weights 2 and 1 and bias 0.5 computes 1 - 1 + 0.5 = 0.5 and keeps it. A unit with weights -2 and 1 computes -1.5, and ReLU turns it into 0. The bend matters, because stacked layers that skip it collapse into one straight-line rule, and spirals need curves.

Our network has 2 inputs, 16 hidden units and 2 outputs, one score per spiral. The hidden layer has 2 × 16 weights plus 16 biases: 48 knobs. The output layer has 16 × 2 weights plus 2 biases: 34. Together 48 + 34 = 82, and PyTorch reports the same count.

Walking Downhill

How do we find good positions for 82 knobs? Picture a hiker in thick fog who feels which way the ground slopes underfoot and takes a small step downhill. That is gradient descent. The loss is the height of the ground, the gradient is the slope, and the rule is new knob = old knob - learning rate × gradient, where the learning rate is the step size.

On our one-knob example the slope is 2w - 4. Start at w = 0 with a learning rate of 0.1: the slope is -4, so the knob moves to 0 + 0.4 = 0.4. At 0.4 the slope is -3.2 and the knob moves to 0.72, then to 0.976. The loss falls 4, 2.56, 1.64, 1.05, and every step is smaller because the slope flattens near the valley.

The learning rate matters a great deal: a tiny step is safe but slow, and a giant step carries the hiker over the valley. With a rate of 1.1 the first step jumps by 4.4, past the target, and the next steps land at -0.88 and 5.456, each swing larger than the last. When a loss curve bounces, a rate that is too large is the first suspect.

Tracing Mistakes Back

Gradient descent needs one slope per knob, and backpropagation delivers all of them in one backwards sweep. Picture a soup that came out too salty: the chef traces backwards from the taste to the broth to the salt, asking how much each step shaped the result. The chain rule of calculus is that tracing: the effect of a knob on the loss is its effect on the prediction times the effect of the prediction on the loss.

Let us trace a tiny network. The input is x = 2, the knobs are w1 = 1, b1 = 1, w2 = 1, b2 = 0, and the target is 6. Forward: the hidden value is 1 × 2 + 1 = 3, the prediction is 3, and the loss is (3 - 6)² = 9. Backward:

  • The slope at the prediction is 2 × (3 - 6) = -6, and b2 adds straight in, so its slope is also -6.
  • The weight w2 multiplies the hidden value 3, so its slope is -6 × 3 = -18.
  • The slope arriving at the hidden value is -6 × w2 = -6, and ReLU passes it along. The bias b1 gets -6, and w1 multiplies the input 2, so its slope is -12.

A quick check: raising w2 by 0.01 drops the loss from 9 to 8.82, which is -18 × 0.01.

PyTorch records the chain during the forward run, and one call to loss.backward() walks it in reverse and fills in a gradient for every knob. This bookkeeping is called autograd. Our demo prints -12, -6, -18 and -6, matching the hand calculation, and a brute-force nudge of each knob by one thousandth agrees, though nudging needs one extra run per knob and backpropagation needs one sweep.

Grids, Probabilities and the Loop

A tensor is a grid of numbers with a shape. Picture an egg tray with four rows and two columns, shape 4 × 2, just like a batch of four points with a horizontal and a vertical position. Multiplying grids follows one rule: the inner sizes must match and the outer sizes survive. A 4 × 2 batch times weights shaped 2 × 16 gives 4 × 16, sixteen hidden values for each point. PyTorch then stretches the bias, shaped 16, across every row, which is called broadcasting. Shape mistakes are the most common error, and the messages are friendly once we read them as two numbers that disagree. We caused three on purpose.

The last layer outputs one raw score per class, called logits. Softmax turns them into positive shares that add up to 100 percent: apply the exponential function to each score, then divide by the total. For scores 2, 1 and 0.1 the exponentials are 7.39, 2.72 and 1.11, the total is 11.21, and the shares are 0.659, 0.242 and 0.099.

To score a prediction we use cross-entropy: minus the natural logarithm of the probability given to the right answer. Picture a weather forecaster: saying 66 percent for rain on a rainy day is a small surprise, and saying 10 percent is a big one. If the first class is right, the loss is -ln(0.659) = 0.417, and if the third is right, it is -ln(0.099) = 2.317. A confident wrong answer costs a lot, and a perfect answer costs zero.

The learning loop is like practicing free throws: shoot, see where it landed, adjust, repeat. It has four moves: forward, loss, backward, step.

Here it is in our raw tensor code:

for step in range(1, steps + 1):
    logits = forward(params, Xtr)                  # 1. forward: make predictions
    loss = softmax_cross_entropy(logits, ytr)      # 2. loss: how far from the targets?
    loss.backward()                                # 3. backward: a gradient for every knob
    with torch.no_grad():                          # 4. step: nudge every knob downhill
        for p in params:
            p -= lr * p.grad                       # new knob = old knob - learning rate * gradient
            p.grad = None                          # clear the gradient for the next round

The update sits inside torch.no_grad() because changing the knobs is bookkeeping we want outside the recorded chain. On the spirals the saved output shows the loss falling from 0.67 at step 0 to 0.37 at step 50, 0.10 at step 300 and 0.06 at step 1,000, with validation accuracy at 98.3 percent, like a hiker reaching the flat valley floor.

Keeping Learning Honest

How do we know a network learned something general instead of memorizing its examples? Picture a student. Practice questions are the training points, and the exam is a set of validation points where we only measure and the knobs stay put. A student who memorized the practice answers earns full marks in practice and a poor mark in the exam, and for a network that gap is called overfitting. We call model.eval() for the exam, so training-only features such as dropout step aside.

To see it, we give a wide network (17,154 knobs) only 50 training points. The plain run memorizes everything, with training accuracy 100 percent. Validation loss is best at step 25, at 0.27, and then climbs to 1.16.

Dropout is a study group where random members stay home at every session, so the group shares the work: with hidden values 2, 4, 6, 8 and a dropout of one half, one draw keeps the first and third and doubles them, giving 4, 0, 12, 0. Weight decay is a gentle leash pulling every knob towards zero: a weight of 2 shrinks by a factor of 1 - 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.99 to 1.98 at each step, and hundreds of steps add up. The four runs show validation loss 1.16 for plain, 0.85 with weight decay, 0.54 with dropout and 0.37 with both. Training accuracy is 100 percent in all four, so the validation score reveals what the training score hides. Stopping at the best moment, around step 25, is early stopping.

Choosing Good Settings

Plain gradient descent is called SGD in PyTorch. Momentum is a ball rolling downhill that remembers recent steps: with momentum 0.9 and rate 0.1 our knob walks 0, 0.4, 1.08, 1.876, 2.617, rolling past the target and back, and arrives sooner overall. AdamW gives every knob its own step size and includes clean weight decay, and most language models train with it. After 300 steps on our network, SGD reaches a loss of 0.53, momentum 0.18 and AdamW 0.12.

Every optimizer needs a learning rate. A sweep with AdamW over nine values shows a crawl at 0.0003, a plateau from 0.01 to 0.3 with validation accuracy near 97 percent, and bouncing at 1, where it drops to 73 percent. Sweep in factors of ten and choose from the middle of the plateau.

A schedule changes the rate during training. A good runner warms up and then glides into the finish: the rate climbs in a straight line for a few warmup steps, then follows half a cosine wave down to zero. In PyTorch a small function goes to LambdaLR. With a bold base rate of 1, a constant rate ends with loss 0.37 and 73 percent validation accuracy, while warmup plus cosine ends with loss 0.05 and 97 percent.

Two last settings. Numbers are stored in a fixed number of bits. Think of a ruler: float32 has a long ruler with fine marks, bfloat16 keeps the long reach with coarser marks, and float16 has finer marks but a short ruler that ends at 65,504. The real output shows pi stored as 3.1415927 in float32 and 3.140625 in both sixteen-bit formats, and 70,000 becoming infinity in float16, which is why bfloat16 became a favorite for training. Every tensor also lives on a device: the CPU by default, or a graphics chip (cuda for Nvidia cards, mps for the graphics chip in some laptops), and x = x.to(device) moves it. Our saved timing shows the CPU ahead for small grids, because starting work on the graphics chip has a cost, and the graphics chip about twice as fast at 4096 × 4096. Timings vary from machine to machine.

The Code and the Lab

The library folder dl_refresher has four files, one idea each: data.py makes the spirals from a seed, so every run makes identical points; raw_mlp.py holds the loop above; module_mlp.py writes the same network with PyTorch building blocks; and report.py saves each run's output. Here is the second version, where a class inherits from nn.Module and PyTorch finds all 82 knobs itself (the forward method simply returns self.net(x)):

class MLP(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, hidden=(16,), dropout=0.0, in_features=2, out_features=2):
        super().__init__()
        layers, width = [], in_features
        for size in hidden:
            layers += [nn.Linear(width, size), nn.ReLU()]   # weights and a bias, then the bend
            if dropout > 0:
                layers.append(nn.Dropout(dropout))
            width = size
        layers.append(nn.Linear(width, out_features))
        self.net = nn.Sequential(*layers)

Its loop has five lines: opt.zero_grad(), forward and F.cross_entropy, loss.backward(), opt.step() and scheduler.step(). On the same spirals the raw version, with 1,000 steps of plain gradient descent, ends with loss 0.06 and 98 percent validation accuracy, and the nn.Module version with AdamW and 500 steps ends with loss 0.08 and 98 percent: the same job in half the steps. Sixteen tests guard the numbers. The project runs with uv from lesson 1: uv sync once, then uv run python -m demos.d06_train_raw, and compare with the expected output saved next to the code.

Here is a challenge for your own lab. Predict the validation accuracy with 2, 4, 8 and 64 hidden units, counting the knobs for each first. The real answer: 2 units give 12 knobs and 55 percent, 4 give 22 knobs and 76 percent, 8 give 42 knobs and 91 percent, 16 give 82 knobs and 98 percent, and 64 give 322 knobs and 99 percent. A network needs enough knobs to bend, and beyond that extra size buys little on a small job. Then change one setting at a time, predict first, run the demo and compare.

To recap: a network is a pile of knobs plus a score called the loss; gradient descent walks the knobs downhill and backpropagation supplies every slope in one sweep; softmax makes probabilities and cross-entropy scores the surprise; and the loop has four moves, forward, loss, backward, step. To learn well, keep a validation set as the exam, calm overfitting with dropout and weight decay, choose AdamW with a rate found by a sweep, add warmup and a cosine glide, and move big jobs to a graphics chip.
