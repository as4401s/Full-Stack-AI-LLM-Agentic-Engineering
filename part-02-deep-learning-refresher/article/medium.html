Parameters and the Loss

Here is the actual thing first. A small network starts with random settings and sees two spirals of points, one arm per class. At first it labels about half of the points correctly, a coin flip. After 1,000 small steps, accuracy, the share of points labeled correctly, climbs from 49 to 98 percent. Only 82 adjustable numbers moved, and this lesson explains the rule that moves them.

How can a network know anything? Picture a mixing desk in a music studio, where the sound depends on every knob position. A network takes numbers in, the input, and gives a number out, the prediction. Inside sits a list of adjustable numbers: the knobs of our picture, properly called parameters, and from here on we say parameters. The smallest network has one parameter, w, and the rule prediction = w × x. With w = 3 and the input x = 2 the prediction is 6, and with w = 5 it is 10. The parameter decides the answer.

How do we tell whether a prediction is good? For each input the data holds the answer we wish for, the target. Let the target be 2 × the input, so the input 1 has the target 2. The miss is the prediction minus the target: at w = 0 the prediction is 0 and the miss is -2. Misses above and below the target are equally bad, so we square the miss, and this squared miss is the loss: one number that says how wrong the prediction is, where smaller is better and zero is perfect. The loss is 4 at w = 0, 1 at w = 1, 0 at w = 2 and 1 at w = 3, a bowl with its bottom at w = 2, where learning wants to go.

What sits inside a network? Small calculators called units. A unit multiplies each input by its own weight, adds the products, adds one more number, the bias, and bends the result with an activation function. The popular one, ReLU, keeps a positive number and replaces a negative one with 0, and the number a unit hands on is its activation. With inputs 0.5 and -1, weights 2 and 1 and bias 0.5, a unit computes 1 - 1 + 0.5 = 0.5 and ReLU keeps it. With weights -2 and 1 the result is -1.5 and ReLU makes it 0. The bend matters, because stacked layers that keep every number as it is collapse into one straight-line rule, and spirals need curves. Weights and biases are the parameters of a unit.

Units sit in groups called layers: the input layer holds our data (two numbers per point), the hidden layer between data and answer has 16 units, and the output layer has 2 units, one score for each spiral. We write the shape of the network as 2, 16, 2, and running it from input to output is the forward pass.

How many parameters does our network have? The hidden layer has 2 × 16 weights plus 16 biases: 48. The output layer has 16 × 2 weights plus 2 biases: 34. Together 48 + 34 = 82, and PyTorch reports the same count.

Walking Downhill

How do we find good values for 82 parameters? Trying all combinations takes far too long, so we need a clue for which way to move each parameter. That clue is the slope: how steeply the loss changes when one parameter changes. For our loss (w - 2)² the slope is 2w - 4, which is -4 at w = 0, 0 at w = 2, the bottom of the bowl, and +2 at w = 3. With many parameters, each has its own slope, and the list of all the slopes is called the gradient. A negative slope means that raising the parameter lowers the loss, so walking against the gradient walks downhill.

Picture a hiker in thick fog who feels the slope underfoot and takes a small step downhill. That is gradient descent. The loss is the height of the ground, the gradient is the slope, and the size of each step is set by a number called the learning rate. The rule is: new parameter = old parameter - learning rate × gradient.

On our one-parameter example, start at w = 0 with a learning rate of 0.1. The slope is -4, so the parameter moves to 0 + 0.4 = 0.4. At 0.4 the slope is -3.2 and the parameter moves to 0.72, then to 0.976. The loss falls 4, 2.56, 1.64, 1.05, and every step is smaller because the slope flattens near the valley.

The learning rate matters: a tiny step is safe but slow, and a giant step carries the hiker over the valley. With a rate of 1.1 the first step jumps to 4.4, past the target, and the next steps land at -0.88 and 5.456, each swing larger than the last.

Tracing Mistakes Back

Gradient descent needs one slope per parameter, and backpropagation delivers all of them in one backwards sweep. Picture a soup that came out too salty: the chef traces backwards from the taste to the broth to the salt. The chain rule of calculus is that tracing: the effect of a parameter on the loss is its effect on the prediction times the effect of the prediction on the loss.

Let us trace a tiny network by hand: two units in a row. The input is x = 2, the parameters are w1 = 1, b1 = 1, w2 = 1 and b2 = 0, and the target is 6. Forward, the first unit computes 1 × 2 + 1 = 3 and ReLU keeps it; the second computes 3 × 1 + 0 = 3, the prediction. The miss is -3 and the loss is 9.

Now we go backwards from the loss:

  • The slope at the prediction is 2 × (3 - 6) = -6, and b2 adds straight in, so its slope is also -6.
  • The weight w2 multiplies the activation 3, so its slope is -6 × 3 = -18.
  • The slope arriving at the activation is -6 × w2 = -6, and ReLU passes it along. The bias b1 gets -6, and w1 multiplies the input 2, so its slope is -12.

A quick check: raising w2 by 0.01 drops the loss from 9 to 8.82, which is -18 × 0.01.

PyTorch records the chain during the forward run, and one call to loss.backward() walks it in reverse and fills in a gradient for every parameter. This bookkeeping is called autograd. Our demo prints -12, -6, -18 and -6, matching the hand calculation, and one sweep replaces a separate trial run for every parameter.

Grids, Probabilities and the Loop

How does a network handle thousands of points at once? It stores numbers in tensors. A tensor is a grid of numbers with a shape, which says how many numbers sit along each direction. Picture an egg tray with four rows and two columns, shape 4 × 2, just like a batch of four points, each with two features: a horizontal and a vertical position.

A whole layer works on a whole batch in one line. The weights of the 16 hidden units form a matrix, a table of numbers, shaped 2 × 16. Multiplying matrices follows one rule: the inner sizes must match and the outer sizes survive, so a 4 × 2 batch times 2 × 16 weights gives 4 × 16, sixteen hidden values for each point. PyTorch then stretches the bias, shaped 16, across every row, which is called broadcasting.

Shape mistakes are the most common error, and their messages read well once we see them as two numbers that disagree.

The last layer outputs one raw score per class, called logits. We want probabilities instead, positive shares that add up to 100 percent, and the tool is softmax: apply the exponential function (the e^x key) to make each score positive, then divide by the total. For scores 2, 1 and 0.1 the exponentials are 7.39, 2.72 and 1.11, the total is 11.21, and the shares are 0.659, 0.242 and 0.099.

To score a probability we use cross-entropy: minus the natural logarithm of the probability given to the right answer. The logarithm (the ln key) reverses the exponential, so ln(1) = 0 and ln(0.5) = -0.69. If the first class is right, the loss is -ln(0.659) = 0.417, and if the third is right, it is 2.317: a confident wrong answer costs a lot, and a perfect answer costs zero.

The learning loop is like practicing free throws: shoot, see where it landed, adjust, repeat. It has four moves: forward, loss, backward, step. On our one-parameter example: prediction 0, loss 4, gradient -4, new w = 0.4.

Here it is in our raw tensor code:

for step in range(1, steps + 1):
    logits = forward(params, Xtr)                  # 1. forward: make predictions
    loss = softmax_cross_entropy(logits, ytr)      # 2. loss: how far from the targets?
    loss.backward()                                # 3. backward: a gradient for every parameter
    with torch.no_grad():                          # 4. step: nudge every parameter downhill
        for p in params:
            p -= lr * p.grad                       # new parameter = old parameter - learning rate * gradient
            p.grad = None                          # clear the gradient for the next round

The update sits inside torch.no_grad() because changing the parameters is bookkeeping outside the recorded chain. We train on 200 points per class and keep 200 more per class aside as a separate check. The saved output shows the loss falling from 0.67 at step 0 to 0.37 at step 50, 0.10 at step 300 and 0.06 at step 1,000, with validation accuracy at 98.3 percent.

Keeping Learning Honest

How do we know a network learned something general instead of memorizing its examples? Picture a student. Practice questions are the training points, and the exam is a set of validation points where we only measure and the parameters stay put. The training loss scores the practice and the validation loss scores the exam. A student who memorized the practice answers earns full marks in practice and a poor mark in the exam, and for a network that gap is called overfitting. We call model.eval() for the exam.

To see it, we give a wide network (17,154 parameters) only 50 training points. The plain run memorizes them, with training accuracy 100 percent. Validation loss is best at step 25, at 0.27, and then climbs to 1.16.

Dropout is a study group where random members stay home at every session, so the group shares the work. In a network it switches off a random share of the hidden units at each training step. With activations 2, 4, 6, 8 and a dropout of one half, one draw keeps the first and third and doubles them, giving 4, 0, 12, 0.

Weight decay is a gentle leash pulling every parameter towards zero. A weight of 2, with a learning rate of 0.1 and a decay setting of 0.1, shrinks by a factor of 1 - 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.99 to 1.98 at each step. The four runs show validation loss 1.16 for plain, 0.85 with weight decay, 0.54 with dropout and 0.37 with both. Training accuracy is 100 percent in all four, so only the validation score reveals the difference. Stopping at the best moment, around step 25, is early stopping.

Choosing Good Settings

The rule that turns gradients into parameter updates is called the optimizer. Plain gradient descent is called SGD in PyTorch, and smarter optimizers use the same gradients differently. Momentum is a ball rolling downhill that remembers recent steps: new step = 0.9 × previous step + the usual gradient step. With rate 0.1 our parameter walks 0, 0.4, 1.08, 1.876, 2.617, rolling past the target and back. AdamW gives every parameter its own step size and includes clean weight decay, and most language models train with it. After 300 steps on our network, SGD reaches a loss of 0.53, momentum 0.18 and AdamW 0.12.

Every optimizer needs a learning rate. A sweep with AdamW over nine values shows a crawl at 0.0003, a plateau from 0.01 to 0.3 with validation accuracy near 97 percent, and bouncing at 1, where accuracy drops to 73 percent. Choose from the middle of the plateau.

A schedule changes the rate during training. A good runner warms up and then glides into the finish: the rate climbs for a few warmup steps, then follows half a cosine wave down to zero, using PyTorch's LambdaLR. With a bold base rate of 1, a constant rate ends with loss 0.37 and 73 percent validation accuracy, while warmup plus cosine ends with loss 0.05 and 97 percent.

Two last settings. Numbers are stored with a fixed number of bits, each a single on-off switch. Think of a ruler: float32 is long with fine marks, bfloat16 keeps the long reach with coarser marks, and float16 has finer marks on a short ruler that ends at 65,504. The real output shows pi as 3.1415927 in float32 and 3.140625 in both sixteen-bit formats, and 70,000 becoming infinity in float16, which is why bfloat16 became a favorite for training. Every tensor also lives on a device: the CPU by default, or a graphics chip (cuda for Nvidia cards, mps for the graphics chip in some laptops), and x = x.to(device) moves it. Our saved timing shows the CPU ahead for small grids and the graphics chip about twice as fast at 4096 × 4096.

The Code and the Lab

The library folder dl_refresher has four files, one idea each: data.py makes the spirals from a seed, so every run makes identical points; raw_mlp.py holds the loop above; module_mlp.py writes the same network with PyTorch building blocks; and report.py saves each run's output. In the second version a class inherits from nn.Module, its layers sit in an nn.Sequential, and PyTorch finds all 82 parameters itself. Its loop has five lines: opt.zero_grad(), forward and F.cross_entropy, loss.backward(), opt.step() and scheduler.step(). The raw version, with 1,000 steps of plain gradient descent, ends with loss 0.06 and 98 percent validation accuracy, and the nn.Module version with AdamW and 500 steps ends with loss 0.08 and 98 percent: the same job in half the steps. The project runs with uv from lesson 1: uv sync once, then uv run python -m demos.d06_train_raw, and compare with the expected output saved next to the code.

Here is a challenge for your own lab. Predict the validation accuracy with 2, 4, 8 and 64 hidden units, counting the parameters for each first. The real answer: 2 units give 12 parameters and 55 percent, 4 give 22 and 76 percent, 8 give 42 and 91 percent, 16 give 82 and 98 percent, and 64 give 322 and 99 percent. A network needs enough parameters to bend, and beyond that extra size buys little on a small job. Then change one setting at a time, predict first and compare.

To recap with the real terms: a network is a function with parameters, the weights and the biases; the loss scores its mistakes; gradient descent changes the parameters against the gradient, backpropagation supplies every gradient in one backwards sweep, and the learning rate sets the step size. The loop has four moves: forward, loss, backward, step. To learn well, keep a validation set as the exam, calm overfitting with dropout and weight decay, and choose AdamW with a rate found by a sweep, warmup and a cosine glide.
